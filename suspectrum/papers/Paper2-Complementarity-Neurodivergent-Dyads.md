# Complementary, Not Just Compatible

## Toward a dyadic model of neurodivergent "squads" in friendship and coworking

*Stage 1 draft (priority-staking Perspective / scoping-review-framing paper). Target venue: Neurodiversity or Autism in Adulthood, with organizational-psychology readership in view. Citations verified against the project literature scout; not a substitute for a final reference check at submission.*

## Abstract

Practitioner and community discourse around neurodivergence increasingly invokes a "squad" intuition, the idea that autistic, ADHD, and AuDHD adults thrive when paired with people whose distinct trait profiles fit their own, so that one person's idea generation meets another's pattern checking. This intuition is complementarity, and it is almost entirely untested. Dyadic research on neurodivergent people has instead examined two other things: whether shared or mismatched neurotype predicts rapport and information transfer, and whether similarity of autistic traits predicts friendship quality. The best evidence points toward similarity, not complementarity, which makes the complementarity claim non-trivial and falsifiable rather than obvious. We organize the field as a matrix crossing level of analysis, predictor type, and relational context, and we show that the dyad-by-complementarity cell in friendship and coworking is empty. We argue that complementarity is a distinct hypothesis worth a competitive test, and we advance a domain-split prediction: similarity might drive felt closeness while complementarity might drive task and role fit. We propose a two-step agenda, a scoping review built on that matrix and a pilot dyadic study using the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model with response-surface analysis, testing main-effect, similarity, and complementarity models against one another across autistic, ADHD, and AuDHD dyads.

## 1. Introduction

Ask an autistic adult how they found their closest collaborator, and the answer often sounds less like "we are the same" and more like "she handles the parts I cannot." The language of the neurodivergent community has settled on a compact metaphor for this, the squad, a small set of people whose strengths interlock. Coaching materials, couples counselors working with AuDHD partners, and workplace consultants all lean on a version of the same story. One person supplies momentum and divergent ideas, another supplies structure and error detection, and the pairing accomplishes what neither could alone. The intuition is everywhere, and it is intuitive precisely because it maps onto something people feel about their own relationships.

The claim underneath the metaphor is complementarity. Two people fit not because their profiles match but because their profiles differ in a way that meshes. This is a specific and testable proposition about dyads, and it matters well beyond a nice framing. If complementarity predicts who works well together and who becomes close, then the advice we give neurodivergent adults about choosing collaborators, friends, and partners should be built around profile fit rather than shared identity. If it does not, then that advice is folklore, and we should say so.

The problem is that the science has not tested it. Research on how neurodivergent people relate to one another has grown quickly, driven in part by the double empathy problem, which reframes social difficulty as a mismatch between people rather than a deficit within the autistic person (Milton, 2012; Milton et al., 2022). That reframing has been generative, though it has also outrun its evidence base in places, and a recent derivation-chain analysis cautions that several of its stronger claims are not yet secured by the data offered for them (Livingston, Hargitai, & Shah, 2025). What the empirical work has actually examined is whether matching or mismatching neurotype changes rapport and information transfer, and, separately, whether similar trait profiles predict better friendships. Neither of these is complementarity. This paper stakes out the gap, argues that filling it is worthwhile and hard, and lays out how to do it.

## 2. What the evidence actually shows

It helps to lay the field out as a matrix. One axis is the level of analysis, individual, dyad, or group. A second axis is the predictor type, binary neurotype match, dimensional trait similarity, or a complementarity interaction between two distinct profiles. A third axis is the relational context, friendship, coworking, or romantic. Reading the literature into these cells reveals both where the density sits and where nothing sits at all.

The binary neurotype-match cluster is the most developed. A body of work initiated by Crompton and colleagues shows that information passes more faithfully along chains of autistic people than along mixed chains, and that pairs of the same neurotype report higher rapport than mixed pairs (Crompton, Ropar, et al., 2020; Crompton, Sharp, et al., 2020). This has held up under scrutiny, including a registered replication and extension (Crompton, Foster, et al., 2025), and it extends from dyads to small groups, where same-neurotype groups again report stronger rapport (Foster et al., 2025). These findings live in the dyad and group rows, under the binary predictor, mostly in brief interactions between strangers rather than sustained relationships. They tell us that neurotype composition matters. They do not tell us anything about how two distinct profiles fit.

The dimensional-similarity column is the next most populated, and it contains the finding this whole paper has to reckon with. Bolis et al. (2021) measured autistic traits continuously in both members of real friendship dyads and found that more similar profiles predicted better friendship quality. Similarity helped. Earlier work on the broader autism phenotype in non-clinical pairs pointed the same direction, with trait configurations relating to how friendships were experienced (Wainer et al., 2013). Recent qualitative and conceptual work on neurodivergent friendship echoes the theme of shared understanding as the connective tissue (Sharman & Seedorf, 2025). So the dimensional column, in the friendship context, at the dyad level, says similarity, not difference.

The coworking context is thinner and newer. Pilot studies have begun to examine neurodiverse collaboration directly, including online task work between autistic and non-autistic adults and simulated remote assembly dyads, and these establish feasibility more than they establish predictors (Wang et al., 2024; Bruen et al., 2025). At the group level, an emerging organizational literature is starting to link individual neurodivergent variation to team processes and effectiveness (Quigley, Meloche, & Roberson, 2026), and systematic reviews of neurodivergence at work document outcomes and barriers without isolating dyadic fit (Vargas-Salas et al., 2025). Methodologically, the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model has entered the field, but so far it has been applied to binary diagnostic status, estimating how one partner's diagnosis relates to both partners' outcomes (Efthimiou et al., 2025). That is an actor-and-partner main-effect use of APIM, not an interaction between two continuous profiles.

Now the empty cell. Nowhere does the literature test a complementarity interaction, a profile-by-profile term where the fit between two people's distinct trait configurations predicts friendship or coworking outcomes, at the dyad level, in ADHD or AuDHD dyads, in sustained relationships or real collaborative work. The binary column is full, the similarity column has its landmark result, and the complementarity column is blank exactly where the community's squad intuition makes its strongest claim. This analysis reveals that the central practitioner hypothesis has never been the subject of a confirmatory dyadic test.

## 3. Why complementarity is a distinct, non-trivial hypothesis

It would be easy to treat complementarity as the obvious next step, a friendly extension of the similarity work. It is not obvious, and that is the point. Bolis et al. (2021) found that similarity helped friendship, which is the opposite of what a naive "opposites attract" reading of the squad metaphor would predict. Any complementarity hypothesis therefore has to survive contact with a real, published result pointing the other way. That is what makes it falsifiable and worth testing rather than assuming.

The older personality literature already warned that complementarity is slippery. Dryer and Horowitz (1997) asked when opposites actually attract and found that people often preferred partners who confirmed their own dispositions, with complementarity mattering only on specific dimensions and often being outweighed by similarity or by perceived similarity. So "complementary profiles fit better" is not a safe default even outside neurodivergence. It is a claim that has failed before and might fail again.

We think the way through is a domain split rather than a single global bet. Similarity and complementarity might govern different outcomes. Similarity might drive felt closeness, mutual understanding, and rapport, the affective side of a relationship, which is consistent with both the double empathy account and the Bolis finding. Complementarity might drive task and role fit, the instrumental side, where two people divide labor because their strengths differ. On this view, the same dyad could feel close because their profiles overlap and also work well because a few key axes diverge, and the friendship and coworking outcomes would load on different predictors. That tension, similarity for closeness and complementarity for task fit, is the contribution. It converts a vague metaphor into two competing, domain-specific predictions.

There is a structural reason to expect that finer dyadic modeling is where the signal lives. Organizational psychology spent decades relating team diversity to team performance using coarse diversity indices, and the yield has been modest. A comprehensive meta-analysis registered report found that team diversity has weak and inconsistent effects on performance overall, varying by diversity type and context (Wallrich, Opara, et al., 2024). Belbin's team-role framework, which sorts people into complementary roles, is popular in practice but empirically underpowered (Belbin, 1981, 1993). The lesson is not that composition is irrelevant. It is that blunt, group-level indices average away the very interactions that matter. A dyad is the smallest unit where a specific profile-by-profile fit can be modeled directly, without being diluted across a whole team, which is an argument for going down to the dyad rather than up to the group.

To keep the trait axes concrete, we anchor one on Kirton's adaption-innovation theory, which contrasts a style that generates novel, boundary-breaking ideas with a style that refines and improves within an existing structure. The idea-generator paired with the pattern-checker is a clean instance of a complementarity axis, and it maps onto the squad language people already use. We invoke KAI as a model for the construct, not as a validated neurodivergence measure, and Belbin only as a cautionary precedent about how such role schemes tend to fare under testing.

## 4. A two-step research agenda

We propose two steps, a review that maps the terrain and a pilot that tests the hypothesis competitively.

Step one is a cross-disciplinary scoping review conducted to JBI guidance and reported with PRISMA-ScR. Its spine is the matrix from Section 2, level of analysis by predictor type by context, and its explicit purpose is to chart which cells are occupied and to headline the empty one. The review has to reach across fields that rarely cite each other, autism and neurodiversity science, organizational and team-composition psychology, personality and interpersonal-complementarity research, and human-factors work on collaboration. A scoping rather than systematic review fits because the question is about the shape and boundaries of a scattered evidence base, not a pooled effect size. The deliverable is a defensible claim that the dyad-by-complementarity cell is empty in friendship and coworking, plus a catalog of the measures and designs each neighboring field already uses, which feeds directly into step two.

Step two is a pilot dyadic study designed from the start as a competitive test of three models rather than a demonstration of one. We would measure both members of each dyad on continuous, non-diagnostic profile axes and on relationship outcomes, then fit three nested accounts. The first is main effects only, where each person's own traits and their partner's traits predict outcomes additively, the actor-and-partner structure already used with binary diagnosis (Efthimiou et al., 2025) but with continuous profiles. The second is similarity, testing whether profile congruence predicts outcomes, the Bolis-style hypothesis. The third is complementarity, an interaction where a specific pattern of divergence between the two profiles predicts outcomes over and above main effects and similarity.

The analytic backbone is the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model extended with response-surface and polynomial regression. This choice is not decorative. Testing "fit" with a naive difference score, subtracting one partner's trait from the other's, is a known trap, because difference scores conflate the level of a trait with the discrepancy between partners and can manufacture or hide effects. Response-surface analysis avoids this by modeling outcomes as a function of both partners' scores and their higher-order terms, letting similarity and complementarity emerge as distinguishable features of a surface rather than being assumed. Crucially, we would define the complementarity construct operationally before collecting data, specifying which axes are expected to be complementary and what pattern on the surface would count as support, so that complementarity and similarity are not quietly relabeled after the fact.

Two design commitments give the study its reach. First, it must include autistic-autistic, ADHD-ADHD, and AuDHD dyads, not only autistic and non-autistic pairs, because the complementarity question is about profile fit within and across neurodivergent presentations, and ADHD and AuDHD dyads are almost absent from the existing dyadic literature. Second, it must include at least one sustained or genuinely collaborative context, an ongoing friendship or a real coworking task, rather than a single brief meeting between strangers. The rapport paradigm has taught us a great deal, but complementarity in task and role fit can only show itself when there is a task and a division of roles to observe over time.

## 5. Measurement notes

Several measurement decisions carry the weight of the whole program. The profile axes should be continuous and non-diagnostic, describing strengths and working styles rather than sorting people into clinical bins, both because that fits the tool this work supports and because dimensional measurement is what makes similarity and complementarity separable in the first place. Where possible we would adapt already-validated instruments rather than mint new ones, drawing friendship-quality and rapport measures from the dyadic work that established them (Bolis et al., 2021; Foster et al., 2025) and collaboration outcomes from emerging coworking studies (Wang et al., 2024; Bruen et al., 2025).

The sharpest hazard is jingle-jangle, calling two different things by one name or one thing by two names. Complementarity is especially exposed here, because it can be quietly confused with dissimilarity, with role differentiation, or with mere trait level. The defense is to define the complementarity construct operationally up front, state which axes are hypothesized to complement, and preregister the surface pattern that would count as evidence, so the construct cannot drift to fit whatever the data show. Response-surface methods support this discipline by forcing similarity and complementarity to be represented as distinct, testable features rather than interchangeable stories.

## 6. Implications and call to action

If the domain-split prediction holds, the practical payoff is immediate. A strengths-based self-discovery tool could tell an autistic, ADHD, or AuDHD adult not only who they resemble but who they might build with, distinguishing the profiles likely to feel like home from the profiles likely to make a project work. Advice to employers assembling neurodiverse teams could move past coarse diversity indices, which the evidence suggests do little on their own (Wallrich et al., 2024), toward modeling the specific dyadic pairings that carry the load. And counselors working with neurodivergent friends and partners would have a defensible reason to talk about fit rather than sameness.

If the prediction fails, that is worth knowing too. A clean competitive test could show that similarity dominates both closeness and task fit, that the squad metaphor is emotionally resonant but instrumentally hollow, and the field would be better for having asked. Either way the value comes from the design, three models set against each other, modeled at the dyad, with the complementarity term specified in advance.

What we are asking for is narrow and concrete. Map the matrix honestly, name the empty cell, and run the small, well-specified dyadic study that can fill it. The squad intuition has organized how a lot of neurodivergent people already live. It deserves either a real test or an honest refusal, and right now it has had neither.
