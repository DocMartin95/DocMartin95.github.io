# Measuring the Right Thing: Masking, Code-Switching, and Minority Stress as Separable Constructs in Racialized Autistic Adults

### Toward a culturally-fair, strengths-based self-discovery tool (the Suspectrum)

*Stage 1 draft (priority-staking Perspective / measurement-agenda paper). Target venue: Autism in Adulthood or Neurodiversity. Citations verified against the project literature scout; not a substitute for a final reference check at submission.*

## Abstract

Autistic adults from racially and ethnically minoritized groups are underdiagnosed, misread, and poorly served by the tools meant to describe them. Three behavioral phenomena plausibly shape how these adults present and how instruments score them: autistic masking, racial-cultural code-switching, and the chronic vigilance produced by minority stress. Each is well-measured on its own. However, they have never been jointly modeled along the racial axis, and code-switching has never been added to a masking and minority-stress model at all. More fundamentally, no study has tested whether the most widely used adult masking measure, the CAT-Q, functions equivalently across racial groups. Race-based differential item functioning has been documented only on childhood, deficit-focused instruments. We argue that collapsing three distinct constructs into a single "masking" score biases every downstream inference, and that a strengths-based test built on this confound might score the most heavily masking people as the least gifted. Before any culturally-fair strengths instrument can be responsibly built, the field must establish measurement invariance across race and demonstrate that the three constructs are empirically separable. We propose a Registered Report measurement agenda: confirmatory factor analysis, tests of configural, metric, and scalar invariance, item-level differential item functioning, and discriminant validity, with community-participatory recruitment and at least 200 participants per major racial group.

## 1. Introduction

The instruments we use to describe autistic people were not built with minoritized autistic adults in mind. This is not a minor calibration problem. When a measure was developed and validated on samples that were overwhelmingly White, and often overwhelmingly diagnosed in childhood through clinical pathways that themselves screen out Black and Brown children, the resulting scores carry the demographic fingerprint of who was studied. Black autistic adults have been largely absent from autism research, a neglect that is both scholarly and consequential, because absence from the evidence base becomes absence from the norms against which everyone else is judged (Malone, Pearson et al., 2022).

The clinical picture makes the stakes concrete. Referral and diagnostic bias mean that racialized autistic people are identified later, or not at all, and are more likely to be misdiagnosed with conduct or mood disorders before autism is ever considered. Diagnostic instruments carry measurable bias here. The ADOS-2, a gold-standard observational tool, shows differential item functioning by race and sex, meaning that children with the same underlying presentation can receive different item scores depending on demographic group (Kalb et al., 2022). If our diagnostic front door is uneven, the tools we build downstream inherit that unevenness unless we deliberately check them.

This paper concerns a different but related problem. We are interested in the self-report measures that increasingly define autistic adulthood, particularly measures of masking, and in the ambition, which we share, to build strengths-based rather than deficit-based tools. We argue that a specific measurement confound stands between the field and any culturally-fair strengths instrument, and that this confound is testable now. Racialized autistic adults live at an intersection where three well-documented behavioral demands overlap. Untangling them is a prerequisite, not an afterthought.

## 2. Three constructs, separately mature

The first construct is autistic masking, sometimes called camouflaging. Masking describes the effortful suppression of autistic characteristics and the performance of non-autistic ones, and it is now measured well. The Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire, the CAT-Q, was developed and validated as a self-report instrument with three subscales, compensation, masking, and assimilation, and it has anchored a large literature on the mental-health costs of camouflaging (Hull et al., 2019). A meta-analytic review of quantification methods confirms that camouflaging can be operationalized in several reliable ways, while also showing how much conceptual heterogeneity still sits beneath the label (Cancino-Barros, Villacura-Herrera & Castillo, 2025). Importantly, camouflaging is increasingly understood not as an individual quirk but as a response to stigma, a strategic management of a devalued social identity (Perry, Mandy, Hull & Cage, 2022).

The second construct is racial-cultural code-switching. Long before autism research adopted the language of masking, scholars of race described how members of minoritized groups shift language, affect, and self-presentation to navigate predominantly White institutions. This is measured through workplace and educational code-switching scales, and it carries its own well-documented cognitive and emotional toll. The parallel to masking is close enough that some scholars have argued the two vocabularies describe overlapping phenomena and should be brought into conversation, while others caution that conflating a disability-related process with a racialized one risks erasing what is distinct about each (Gerlach-Houck & DeThorne, 2023, and the ASHA response letter). That very debate signals the measurement problem we raise here. If experts disagree about whether these are the same thing, the field cannot keep assuming a single self-report score captures both.

The third construct is minority-stress vigilance. The minority stress model holds that members of stigmatized groups face chronic, socially-based stressors, expectations of rejection, concealment, internalized stigma, that operate above and beyond ordinary stress and degrade health. Botha and Frost extended this model to autistic people, showing that minority stress processes predict autistic adults' mental health (Botha & Frost, 2020). This construct is captured through discrimination scales and, increasingly, autism-specific minority-stress measures. It is conceptually distinct from the other two. A person can be vigilant for rejection without actively masking or code-switching in a given moment, and the vigilance itself, the constant scanning of a room for threat, has costs that are not reducible to performance.

Each of these three is mature on its own terms. Each has instruments, validation studies, and a body of findings. The problem begins when they meet in one body.

## 3. The measurement gap

Here is what has and has not been done. Masking and minority stress have been jointly modeled, and the result is instructive. White and colleagues examined minority stress, camouflaging, and mental-health outcomes together in transgender and non-binary autistic adults, treating gender-based minority stress and autistic camouflaging as related but separable predictors (White, Ixqe, Goodall & Gillespie-Smith, 2024). This is the template we want to generalize. It shows that two of our three constructs can be placed in one model, on one minoritized axis, and studied for their joint and distinct contributions. Similar joint modeling has touched the sexual-orientation axis.

However, this has never been done on the racial axis. No study has jointly modeled masking and minority stress for Black, Latine, Asian, or Indigenous autistic adults as racial groups, and none has added code-switching as a third predictor. The qualitative literature makes clear that the phenomenon is real and pressing. Black autistic young adults describe navigating race and autism as intersecting identities that compound one another (Davis, Solomon & Belcher, 2022). Autistic people of color describe being stuck in the middle, subject to an intersectional stigma that neither the autism community nor their racial community fully holds (Jones, Botha & Sasson, 2025). Pearson and Rose's minority-group model of neurodiversity gives this a theoretical frame, locating masking within a structure of neurotypical privilege rather than individual deficit (Pearson & Rose). The lived-experience scholarship is ahead of the measurement science, and the measurement science needs to catch up.

The deepest part of the gap is this. No study has tested differential item functioning or measurement invariance across race or ethnicity for the CAT-Q, or for any adult masking or strengths self-report instrument. Differential item functioning, DIF, asks a precise question. Holding the underlying trait constant, do people from different groups respond differently to a given item? If a masking item behaves differently for Black respondents than for White respondents at the same true level of masking, that item is not measuring the same thing across groups, and any comparison of scores is compromised. This has simply never been checked for adult masking measures.

Where race DIF has been examined, it appears on childhood, deficit-focused scales, and it is often present. ADHD symptom ratings show differential item functioning across gender, age, and race or ethnicity (Lawson et al., 2020). The Autism Spectrum Rating Scales show DIF as well (McClain, Harris, Schwartz & Golson, 2021). The ADOS-2 shows race and sex DIF (Kalb et al., 2022). So the field knows how to run these analyses, knows that demographic DIF is common, and has documented it repeatedly, but only on instruments that count deficits in children. The strengths-based, adult, self-report instruments that the neurodiversity movement is now building have received no such scrutiny. This asymmetry is itself a finding. We check our tools for bias when they pathologize, and we trust them when they affirm.

## 4. Why it matters

The goal that motivates this paper is a culturally-fair, non-diagnostic, strengths-based self-discovery tool for autistic, ADHD, and AuDHD adults, which we are developing as Suspectrum. Interest in autistic strengths is growing, and adults readily report distinctive talents when asked (Lampinen et al., 2026). A strengths inventory is a hopeful idea. It can also go badly wrong.

Consider the failure mode directly. A strengths self-report asks respondents to endorse capacities and interests. A person who has spent decades masking has learned to suppress and disguise exactly the intense focus, the pattern sensitivity, the direct communication that a strengths test hopes to detect. Someone code-switching in the testing context might present a flattened, cautious version of themselves. Someone in a state of minority-stress vigilance might under-endorse anything that marks them as different, because being marked as different has been costly. The predictable result is that a naive strengths test scores the most heavily masking, most heavily surveilled people lowest. The people whose traits are most hidden receive the lowest estimates of their gifts. This is the opposite of what a strengths tool is for, and it would reproduce, in affirming language, the same demographic gradient that deficit tools produce.

DIF is the technical definition of the fairness we are after. Fairness here is not a slogan. It is the measurable property that an item conveys the same information about the underlying trait regardless of group membership. Establishing invariance is what licenses us to compare scores, to build norms, and to tell an individual something true about themselves rather than something about their demographic position. Without it, a strengths score is confounded with the very masking and minority stress it fails to measure separately.

We want to be careful about causation. Trauma, crisis, and the exhaustion of chronic masking often precede the moment an adult recognizes their neurodivergence, and late diagnosis frequently follows a period of collapse. We frame these experiences as revealing pre-existing traits, unmasking what was always there, rather than as causing neurodivergence. A person does not become autistic because masking failed. Rather, the failure of masking makes visible what masking concealed. Any instrument built here should honor that direction of explanation, and should treat heavy masking as a reason a trait is hidden from measurement, never as evidence the trait is absent.

## 5. A proposed measurement agenda

We propose a staged, pre-registered study, ideally as a Registered Report, so that the analysis plan is fixed before data are seen and the result is publishable whatever it shows.

**Constructs and instruments.** Collect autistic masking with the CAT-Q, racial-cultural code-switching with an established workplace or educational code-switching scale, and minority-stress vigilance with discrimination measures and an autism-specific minority-stress instrument. Include a preliminary strengths inventory drawing on recent work on self-reported autistic strengths. Critically, also collect social anxiety and impression-management or socially-desirable-responding measures, because these are the constructs most likely to masquerade as masking and to threaten discriminant validity.

**Analysis sequence.** First, fit a confirmatory factor analysis specifying the three constructs as distinct latent factors, and test it against plausible collapsed alternatives. Second, test measurement invariance across race in the standard nested sequence, configural invariance to establish the same factor structure holds, then metric invariance to establish equal loadings, then scalar invariance to establish equal intercepts, which is what comparing means requires. Third, move to the item level and test differential item functioning across racial groups using item response theory and ordinal logistic regression, so that specific biased items can be identified, quantified, and flagged. Fourth, test discriminant validity, showing that masking, code-switching, and minority-stress vigilance separate from one another and from social anxiety and impression management, rather than loading onto one general factor.

**Sampling.** Invariance and DIF analyses are demanding of data. We target at least 200 participants per major racial group, enough to estimate group-specific parameters with reasonable stability. This is a floor, not a ceiling, and the smallest groups will need deliberate oversampling.

**Community-participatory recruitment.** The scholarly neglect of Black autistic adults will not be undone by a research team that treats them as subjects to be sampled. Recruitment, item review, and interpretation should be participatory, involving autistic people of color as advisors and co-analysts, so that items are read for cultural meaning before they are read for statistical function. This is both an ethical commitment and a methodological one, because the community is best positioned to say what an item might mean differently across groups, which is the human-level source of DIF.

## 6. Implications and call to action

The neurodiversity movement has won an important argument, that autistic people have strengths worth naming and tools worth building. The risk now is that we build those tools on the same uninspected foundations that made the deficit tools unfair, and that we do so in the affirming language that makes bias harder to see. A strengths test that quietly penalizes the most masked, most surveilled, most minoritized autistic adults would be a betrayal dressed as liberation.

We are not asking the field to abandon strengths-based measurement. We are asking it to do one thing first. Establish whether existing masking items measure the same construct across racial groups, and whether masking, code-switching, and minority-stress vigilance are empirically separable in the bodies that carry all three at once. These are answerable questions. The statistical machinery exists and has been applied to deficit scales for years. The lived-experience scholarship has already told us the phenomenon is real. What remains is to run the invariance and DIF analyses on the affirming, adult, self-report tools that will shape how a generation of autistic adults come to understand themselves. Suspectrum cannot be culturally fair by intention. It can only be culturally fair by measurement, and measurement is something we can test before we build.
